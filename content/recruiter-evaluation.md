# Recruiter Evaluation & Improvement Suggestions

*An honest, external read on the portfolio as it stands — where it's strong, where it's exposed, and what would move the needle most before this goes in front of a bank, investor, procurement office, or executive recruiter.*

## Overall Read

This is a genuinely unusual candidate profile: a founder who has run P&L, negotiated corporate contracts, carried full regulatory compliance, *and* personally built the software systems her businesses run on. That combination — operator plus builder — is rare and, positioned correctly, is the single strongest thing in this portfolio. Most operations executives can't build; most builders have never run a fleet payroll under BIR audit risk. That's the headline, and the current site leads with it well.

The zero-major-compliance-violations record is the second strongest asset here. In transport/logistics, that single fact does more credibility work than almost any other claim in the document — it's specific, checkable in principle, and unglamorous in a way that reads as true rather than marketed.

## Strengths

- **Specificity over adjectives.** The resume avoids "results-driven self-starter" filler almost entirely and instead names real entities, real dates, real branch counts (4 branches, 16+ vehicles, since 2013). That specificity is what makes it read as executive-grade rather than templated.
- **Ownership clarity.** The Owner/Manager/Client/Consultant distinction across the five business relationships is unusually disciplined — most self-authored portfolios blur "client" and "partner" to sound bigger. Keeping RBJ as a client rather than inflating it to "Managing Partner" is the right call and will hold up under any diligence question.
- **The Featured Projects section is a genuine differentiator** for someone positioning as an ERP/business-systems strategist — most operations executives have nothing to show here at all.

## Risks and Gaps — What a Skeptical Reader Will Push On

1. **Zero quantified results on all four Featured Projects.** This is the single biggest weakness right now. "Built a transport management system" is a claim; "cut dispatch admin time by 6 hours a week" is evidence. Right now every project ends on a placeholder. A recruiter or investor will notice this pattern immediately and it undercuts the credibility of the whole section — fill in even one real number and the rest reads more credibly by association.
2. **No proof artifacts for the software claims.** Four "engineered systems" with a modern stack (React/Next.js/Supabase) is a strong claim with nothing to click on. Before this goes to anyone technical — an investor's tech advisor, a recruiter for a systems-facing role — there should be *something* verifiable: a live demo, a screen recording, a GitHub repo (even private, shareable on request), or screenshots embedded directly in the case studies. Right now the claim is asserted, not shown.
3. **No third-party corroboration anywhere.** No testimonials, no named references, no LinkedIn recommendations. For a document meant for banks and procurement offices, a single sentence from a corporate client ("their van showed up on time for 18 months straight") would outweigh several paragraphs of self-description.
4. **The technical skill list is broad relative to what's demonstrated.** Python, PHP, MySQL, Git, GitHub, and Vercel are all listed alongside React/Supabase/SQL, but none of the four case studies mention them. Either surface where each is actually used, or trim the list to what the projects support — a skills list that outruns the portfolio invites exactly the kind of technical screening question that's hardest to recover from in an interview.
5. **Missing contact infrastructure.** No LinkedIn URL, no portfolio/website URL anywhere. For someone positioning at this level, an incomplete or absent LinkedIn profile is a bigger credibility gap than any line item on the resume — it's usually the first thing a recruiter or diligence contact checks.
6. **Revenue and client-count figures are both blank.** Understandable if the numbers aren't ready to disclose, but "corporate clients served" and "revenue managed" are exactly the two figures a bank or investor conversation will ask for first. Even a range ("₱X–Y annual revenue," "8–12 active corporate accounts") is stronger than a dash.

## Concrete Next Steps, in Priority Order

1. Add one real, specific result to each Featured Project — even directional ("reduced late-dispatch incidents," "cut monthly reconciliation from 3 days to 1") is far stronger than a placeholder.
2. Get a LinkedIn profile live and linked from every document — this is the highest-leverage fifteen minutes available right now.
3. Add one proof artifact per system (screenshot, short screen recording, or repo link) to the Projects section.
4. Ask one corporate client, and one of the two consulting clients (RBJ or a BlueScale engagement), for two sentences of testimonial. Two real quotes will outweigh the entire "Testimonials — reserved" placeholder section.
5. Commit to a revenue range and a client-count figure, even approximate, rather than leaving both blank on the executive dashboard.

## What Not to Change

Don't add more adjectives, more competency buzzwords, or a longer skills list to compensate for the gaps above — none of that is what's missing. What's missing is evidence for the claims already made. This portfolio's credibility ceiling is set by its weakest unverified claim, not raised by its longest section.
